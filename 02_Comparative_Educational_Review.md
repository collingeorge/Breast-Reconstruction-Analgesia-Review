## Purpose

This review examines published literature on analgesic and anesthetic approaches in post-mastectomy breast implant revision surgery, with particular attention to opioid-sparing, multimodal, and regional anesthesia frameworks as discussed in current perioperative literature. The goal is to understand how the literature characterizes the comparative considerations, potential advantages, reported limitations, and patient-specific factors associated with different perioperative analgesic strategies in this setting.

This is not an attempt to synthesize a clinical pathway. It is an effort to understand the reasoning clinicians and researchers use when evaluating perioperative options — and to analyze how evidence quality, patient heterogeneity, and institutional variability shape those discussions.

---

## Methods Note

This work is a narrative literature review rather than a systematic review or formal meta-analysis. No PRISMA framework was applied, and source identification was not exhaustive.

Literature was identified through searches of PubMed using combinations of terms including "breast reconstruction," "implant revision," "regional anesthesia," "PECS block," "opioid-sparing anesthesia," "opioid-free anesthesia," "PONV," "multimodal analgesia," and "enhanced recovery after surgery," supplemented by society guidance and reference tracking. Priority was given to society guidance, systematic reviews, randomized trials, and perioperative publications most directly relevant to breast surgery and reconstruction settings. The documented search strings used for the companion manuscript are reported in `George-BreastRecon-Review-v8.md`.

Evidence quality across sources is heterogeneous. Where possible, guideline-level evidence is distinguished from trial-level evidence and expert opinion, though this review does not apply a formal evidence-grading framework. Where direct evidence specific to breast reconstruction revision was limited, adjacent surgical literature was interpreted cautiously and identified as extrapolative where applicable. Readers should consult primary sources and institutional resources for any clinical purpose.

## Background and Clinical Context

Post-mastectomy breast implant revision surgery encompasses a range of procedures including capsular contracture management, implant exchange, pocket revision, and infection-related explantation and reimplantation. These procedures present perioperative management considerations that have attracted substantial literature interest, particularly regarding analgesic strategy, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) prevention, and recovery quality.

The published literature on this topic intersects several active areas of perioperative medicine: enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways, opioid stewardship, regional anesthesia expansion, and multimodal analgesia. Understanding how these intersect in the context of breast reconstruction revision is the organizing question of this review.

Perioperative management in this setting involves collaboration across surgical, anesthesia, and nursing teams. Decisions about analgesic strategy, regional technique, and postoperative monitoring are highly individualized and institution-dependent. The discussion below reflects how these decisions are framed in the published literature — not how they should be made in any specific clinical context.

## Section 1: Multimodal Analgesia — Conceptual Framework

### 1.1 General Principles in Published Literature

ERAS-aligned literature commonly describes multimodal analgesia as a foundational concept in perioperative pain management (Temple-Oberle et al., 2017; Jacobs et al., 2020). The core principle, as articulated in these publications, is that combining analgesic agents with different mechanisms of action may reduce reliance on any single agent class — including opioids — while maintaining or improving analgesia quality.

Agents commonly discussed in the multimodal analgesia literature for breast surgery settings include acetaminophen, NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors, alpha-2 agonists (dexmedetomidine), NMDA receptor antagonists (ketamine), sodium channel modulators (intravenous lidocaine), regional anesthetics, and glucocorticoids (dexamethasone). The evidence base for individual agents varies considerably in depth and consistency across published studies.

### 1.2 ERAS Society Guidance

An ERAS Society–endorsed consensus review for breast reconstruction recommends opioid-sparing perioperative medications, anesthetic techniques that decrease PONV and pain, minimal preoperative fasting, prevention of intraoperative hypothermia, and early mobilization (Temple-Oberle et al., 2017). The review notes that most of its recommendations rest on lower-level or extrapolated evidence. The PROSPECT guideline for oncological breast surgery similarly recommends basic non-opioid analgesics, with regional techniques considered for major breast surgery and opioids reserved for rescue (Jacobs et al., 2020). Applicability to specific patients and procedures requires individualized clinical judgment by the treating team and integration with local institutional protocols.

### 1.3 Evidence Heterogeneity

A limitation noted across the literature is heterogeneity in study populations, outcome definitions, analgesic combinations, and institutional practices. Findings from one institutional or procedural context do not translate automatically to others. This review reflects that heterogeneity and does not attempt to resolve it through synthesis of a single approach.

## Section 2: Regional Anesthesia for Breast Surgery

### 2.1 Pectoral Nerve Blocks (PECS I and II)

The PECS I block was first described by Blanco in 2011, and the PECS II ("modified Pecs") block in 2012 (Blanco, 2011; Blanco et al., 2012). Both have been the subject of substantial literature interest in breast surgery analgesia. PECS I targets the lateral and medial pectoral nerves in the fascial plane between pectoralis major and pectoralis minor. PECS II adds a deeper injection intended to block at least the pectoral nerves, the intercostobrachial nerve, intercostal nerves III–VI, and the long thoracic nerve (Blanco et al., 2012).

Meta-analyses and trials describe opioid-sparing effects associated with PECS blocks in breast cancer surgery, although effect size, certainty, and generalizability vary across studies (Hussain et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2020; Bashandy & Abbas, 2015). A SAMBA practice advisory recommends PECS blocks over systemic analgesia alone for breast-conserving surgery but does not recommend them over surgeon-performed local infiltration (Ardon et al., 2022). Dexamethasone as a perineural adjuvant has been reported in a meta-analysis of peripheral nerve block trials to prolong analgesia, although results were highly heterogeneous; evidence specific to PECS techniques is limited relative to the broader peripheral nerve block literature (Albrecht et al., 2015).

### 2.2 Serratus Anterior Plane Block

The serratus anterior plane (SAP) block has been described as an alternative or complement to PECS II, with reported coverage of the lateral chest wall. Published evidence for the SAP block includes data from thoracic surgery populations — including Kim et al. (Anesthesia & Analgesia, 2018), a randomized trial that evaluated the block in video-assisted thoracic surgery patients — and extrapolation to breast reconstruction revision settings remains indirect. A network meta-analysis in breast cancer surgery found SAP, PECS II, paravertebral, erector spinae plane, and rhomboid intercostal blocks all associated with lower pain and opioid use than no block, with very low strength of evidence for ranking among them (An et al., 2023).

### 2.3 Reported Considerations and Limitations

Published literature discusses several considerations relevant to regional anesthesia in this setting.

Local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) is a recognized risk with large-volume regional techniques. ASRA Pain Medicine's practice advisory and checklist on LAST prevention, recognition, and management serve as widely used reference frameworks for this topic, and include lipid emulsion resuscitation as part of preparedness (Neal et al., 2018; Neal et al., 2021).

Bilateral procedures require attention to cumulative local anesthetic dosing. The literature discusses weight-based dosing principles and the importance of accounting for combined systemic and regional local anesthetic exposure when intravenous lidocaine infusions are used concurrently with fascial plane blocks, because both contribute to the total local anesthetic load (Neal et al., 2018). No doses are given in this document.

Patient-specific factors including coagulopathy, anticoagulation status, local infection, and anatomic variation are described in the literature as relevant to regional anesthesia eligibility, with ASRA Pain Medicine's antithrombotic guidelines serving as a primary reference framework (Horlocker et al., 2018).

## Section 3: Opioid-Sparing and Opioid-Free General Anesthesia

### 3.1 Conceptual Framing in the Literature

Opioid-free anesthesia (OFA) and opioid-sparing anesthesia (OSA) are terms used with some variability across publications to describe approaches that minimize or eliminate intraoperative opioid administration in favor of non-opioid analgesic combinations (Beloeil, 2019). Beloeil describes OFA as feasible but notes that the literature on clinically meaningful patient benefit, and on the side effects or complications that may accompany it, is still scarce.

The rationale discussed in published literature includes potential reduction in opioid-related adverse effects — particularly PONV, respiratory depression, and opioid-induced hyperalgesia — as well as alignment with opioid stewardship principles. Whether these benefits are realized in specific clinical contexts depends on patient selection, analgesic combination, and institutional factors.

### 3.2 Intravenous Analgesic Adjuncts

Several intravenous agents are discussed in the perioperative literature as components of opioid-sparing frameworks:

**Dexmedetomidine** is an alpha-2 agonist with sedative, anxiolytic, and analgesic-sparing properties (Weerink et al., 2017). A meta-analysis of perioperative alpha-2 agonists reported reduced 24-hour morphine consumption with dexmedetomidine and an increased risk of postoperative bradycardia (Blaudszun et al., 2012). The balance of potential analgesic benefit against hemodynamic effects is described as patient-specific and requiring attending-level clinical judgment.

**Intravenous lidocaine infusion** is discussed as having analgesic and opioid-sparing properties in several surgical contexts (McCarthy et al., 2010). Meta-analytic evidence shows benefit mainly in abdominal surgery; toxic plasma levels were observed in some trials, and the authors called for dose and safety to be established (Vigneault et al., 2011). Evidence specific to breast surgery is limited. Because IV lidocaine adds to the total local anesthetic load, its combination with regional blocks is relevant to LAST risk (see Section 2.3).

**Ketamine** at subanesthetic doses is an NMDA antagonist. A Cochrane review concluded that perioperative IV ketamine probably reduces postoperative analgesic consumption and pain intensity across a range of operations, including mastectomy (Brinck et al., 2018). Cautions commonly described for ketamine include psychiatric history such as psychosis and conditions in which increases in blood pressure or heart rate would be hazardous.

### 3.3 Propofol Total Intravenous Anesthesia

Propofol-based TIVA is discussed in published literature as associated with reduced PONV compared with volatile anesthetic maintenance, and consensus PONV guidelines address anesthetic technique as part of reducing baseline PONV risk (Gan et al., 2020). This property is cited in ERAS-aligned perioperative literature as a rationale for TIVA consideration in settings where PONV prevention is a clinical priority, though the attending team determines appropriateness for individual patients.

## Section 4: Opioid-Inclusive Approaches — Reported Context

### 4.1 Continued Role in Selected Populations

Published literature does not characterize opioid-inclusive anesthesia as categorically suboptimal in all contexts. Procedure-specific guidance for oncological breast surgery reserves opioids for postoperative rescue analgesia rather than excluding them (Jacobs et al., 2020). In the author's reading, opioid-containing approaches remain a recognized option in populations such as patients on chronic opioid therapy, those with multiple contraindications to non-opioid adjuncts, and those in whom opioid-sparing strategies have previously proven inadequate.

Inadequate pain management in patients with opioid tolerance carries its own patient safety implications, and coordination with chronic prescribers and pain management specialists is generally described as important in these contexts.

### 4.2 Opioid Minimization Within Conventional Approaches

Even within opioid-containing frameworks, published ERAS and fast-track literature commonly discusses opioid minimization principles, including reduced opioid exposure where feasible, combination with non-opioid multimodal adjuncts, and prophylaxis against side effects such as pain, nausea, and vomiting (Kehlet & Wilmore, 2008; White et al., 2007; Temple-Oberle et al., 2017). This framing reflects the broader opioid stewardship orientation present throughout contemporary perioperative literature.

### 4.3 PONV in Opioid-Inclusive Contexts

Postoperative opioid use is one of the four predictors in the Apfel simplified risk score, alongside female sex, history of motion sickness or PONV, and non-smoking (Apfel et al., 1999). The Apfel score is among the most widely referenced PONV risk stratification tools in perioperative guidelines, and consensus publications describe multi-agent PONV prophylaxis as appropriate for patients at moderate to high risk (Gan et al., 2020).

## Section 5: Comparative Considerations Reported in the Literature

### 5.1 How Authors Frame the Comparison

Published literature discussing the comparison between opioid-sparing and opioid-inclusive approaches generally does not present a binary choice, but rather a spectrum of approaches calibrated to patient-specific factors. The framing in ERAS-aligned and procedure-specific publications is that regional and multimodal approaches are broadly discussed as potentially advantageous where feasible, with opioids retained for rescue or when opioid-sparing approaches are contraindicated, declined by patients, or technically not possible (Jacobs et al., 2020; Temple-Oberle et al., 2017).

### 5.2 Factors Discussed as Relevant in the Literature

Across ERAS-aligned and perioperative review literature, commonly discussed patient and procedural factors relevant to analgesic approach considerations in this setting include (Temple-Oberle et al., 2017; Jacobs et al., 2020; Gan et al., 2020; Horlocker et al., 2018):

- PONV risk profile and individual risk factors
- Coagulopathy or anticoagulation status as relevant to regional anesthesia feasibility
- Chronic opioid use and tolerance
- Comorbidities including obstructive sleep apnea, hepatic dysfunction, renal impairment, and cardiac conditions
- Procedure complexity and anticipated dissection extent
- Patient preferences regarding needle-based procedures
- Institutional formulary, local anesthetic availability, and regional anesthesia expertise

These factors are discussed in the literature as informing individualized clinical decision-making under attending supervision. They are presented here as educational context, not as a decision framework.

### 5.3 Recovery Outcomes Discussed in the Literature

Published studies examining opioid-sparing perioperative approaches in breast surgery contexts generally report outcomes including postoperative pain scores, PONV incidence, opioid consumption, time to discharge readiness, and patient satisfaction. Effect sizes and confidence in findings vary across studies depending on design quality, population characteristics, and outcome definitions. Readers are directed to primary sources for specific outcome data.

### 5.4 Important Uncertainties

Several important uncertainties are noted across the literature:

- Many studies are conducted at centers with specific regional anesthesia expertise; generalizability to other settings is uncertain
- Patient populations vary substantially across studies in terms of comorbidity burden, procedure complexity, and baseline opioid exposure
- Outcome definitions — particularly for PONV and "opioid-free" — are inconsistent across publications
- Long-term outcomes beyond the immediate perioperative period are less well-characterized
- Optimal combinations of multimodal agents and their relative contributions remain subjects of ongoing investigation

## Section 6: PONV — Literature Summary

PONV is discussed extensively in the perioperative breast surgery literature as a clinically important outcome affecting patient comfort, recovery quality, and resource utilization. The Apfel simplified risk score is among the most widely cited risk stratification tools in this context (Apfel et al., 1999; Gan et al., 2020).

Published consensus guidelines describe a tiered prophylaxis approach in which the number and combination of preventive agents are scaled to patient risk level. Agents discussed include 5-HT3 antagonists, glucocorticoids, neurokinin-1 antagonists, and transdermal scopolamine, among others. The general principle across published rescue antiemetic discussions is to avoid repeating the same drug class in sequential rescue attempts (Gan et al., 2020).

The relationship between anesthetic technique and PONV is discussed in terms of both opioid minimization and propofol TIVA, both of which are associated with reduced PONV in published literature compared with opioid-containing volatile anesthesia in high-risk populations (Gan et al., 2020).

## Section 7: Special Population Considerations in the Literature

Published perioperative literature and safety guidance commonly identify several patient populations as requiring additional consideration in analgesic planning:

**Elderly and frail patients** are generally described as having increased sensitivity to sedative and analgesic agents, higher delirium risk, and reduced hepatic and renal clearance, making dose consideration and postoperative monitoring important. Processed EEG monitoring is discussed in some publications as potentially useful for anesthetic depth titration in this population.

**Patients with obstructive sleep apnea** are a population in whom opioid-related respiratory depression risk may be elevated. The American Society of Anesthesiologists has published practice guidelines on the perioperative management of these patients, including postoperative monitoring (American Society of Anesthesiologists Task Force on Perioperative Management of Patients with Obstructive Sleep Apnea, 2014).

**Patients on chronic opioid therapy** are generally discussed in terms of tolerance, opioid-induced hyperalgesia, and the importance of perioperative analgesic planning that accounts for baseline opioid requirements. Coordination with prescribing clinicians is described as important in this context.

**Patients with opioid use disorder in recovery** are generally discussed with attention to the ethical dimensions of opioid exposure in the perioperative setting, the potential value of opioid-sparing approaches where feasible, and the importance of involving addiction medicine or recovery support resources.

These population-specific considerations are summarized for educational purposes only.

## Section 8: Limitations of This Review

This review has several important limitations that readers should consider.

This is a narrative literature review, not a systematic review. Source identification was not exhaustive, and no formal evidence-grading framework was applied. The review reflects the author's educational synthesis of available literature and should not be treated as a comprehensive or authoritative evidence assessment.

The author is a premedical student without clinical licensure or authority to provide clinical guidance. Interpretations reflect educational engagement with published sources rather than professional clinical judgment.

Evidence quality across the sources reviewed is heterogeneous. Guideline recommendations, randomized trial findings, observational data, and expert opinion are discussed without consistent formal grading. Readers should consult primary sources and qualified clinicians for any clinical purpose.

Institutional variability is substantial. Perioperative practices, formulary availability, regional anesthesia expertise, and patient populations vary across institutions in ways that significantly affect the applicability of any published findings. Nothing in this review should be read as applicable to any specific institution, clinical context, or patient population.

This review reflects literature identified through 2025, with references re-verified in October 2026. The perioperative medicine literature evolves continuously, and subsequent publications may modify or supersede the discussions summarized here.

## Conclusion

The published perioperative literature on breast reconstruction revision surgery reflects an active and evolving discussion about analgesic strategy, PONV prevention, regional anesthesia integration, and recovery optimization. Several themes recur across sources: the potential advantages of opioid-sparing and multimodal approaches in appropriately selected patients, the importance of regional anesthesia expertise and patient-specific selection in realizing those advantages, the retained role of opioid-containing approaches in specific clinical contexts, and the centrality of individualized attending-level clinical judgment in all perioperative decision-making.

This educational review is intended to synthesize those themes for academic learning purposes. It does not resolve the clinical questions it discusses, advocate for specific approaches, or substitute for the expertise of the licensed clinicians who bear responsibility for perioperative care.

## Abstract

**Background:** Breast reconstruction surgery raises perioperative analgesic questions across five sequential decision domains: analgesic modality, regional anesthetic technique, adjuvant pharmacology, patient risk stratification and communication, and postoperative recovery measurement. These domains are usually studied separately. The author did not identify a prior review that mapped evidence across all five domains together, particularly with respect to breast implant revision surgery as a procedurally distinct, under-studied subpopulation.

**Objective:** To describe the scope and nature of published evidence for perioperative analgesic strategies across five decision domains in adults undergoing breast reconstruction surgery, and to describe evidence gaps relevant to breast implant revision surgery.

**Sources:** A documented PubMed/MEDLINE search (five domain-specific strings), supplemented by targeted retrieval of society guidance and backward reference tracking. Sources were selected by a single author; screening counts were not recorded. Forty-nine published sources are cited in this manuscript.

**Findings:** Two meta-analyses of randomized trials in breast cancer surgery report that pectoral nerve (PECS) blocks reduce 24-hour postoperative opioid consumption compared with general anesthesia or systemic analgesia alone. Sun et al. (2020; 13 RCTs, 940 patients) reported a weighted mean difference of −11.88 mg morphine equivalents (95% CI −15.50 to −8.26) with very high heterogeneity (I² = 99.5%). Hussain et al. (2019; 14 RCTs, 887 patients) reported a weighted mean difference of −30.5 mg oral morphine (95% CI −42.2 to −18.8) for PECS II versus systemic analgesia and no statistically significant difference versus paravertebral block. These estimates are reported separately and were not pooled by this review. The author proposes, as a hypothesis only, that the measured opioid-sparing effect of PECS blocks may depend on the anesthetic background (for example, propofol total intravenous anesthesia [TIVA] versus volatile anesthesia); the available trials do not allow this to be tested. Meta-analyses support small-to-moderate opioid-sparing effects for dexamethasone, ketamine, alpha-2 agonists, magnesium, and IV lidocaine in mixed surgical populations, with little breast-reconstruction-specific evidence. Communication research documents gaps in informed decision-making and consent-form readability in general medical and surgical settings. Recovery-measurement research documents that recovery instruments vary widely in scope. No study specific to breast implant revision was identified in any domain; the closest direct evidence comes from primary aesthetic breast augmentation.

**Conclusion:** The strongest evidence in this literature concerns regional analgesia (meta-analyses of RCTs, with substantial heterogeneity), and the weakest concerns integrated communication and outcome measurement. Conclusions about implant revision are inferences, not established findings, and would need revision-specific trials.

## 1. Introduction

Breast reconstruction surgery encompasses immediate and delayed post-mastectomy reconstruction, implant-based reconstruction with tissue expander or direct-to-implant technique, autologous reconstruction, and revision procedures including capsular contracture management, implant exchange, pocket revision, and infection-related explantation with reimplantation. These procedures present perioperative management considerations that intersect five areas of active scholarship: analgesic modality selection, regional anesthesia technique, adjuvant pharmacology, patient risk stratification and communication quality, and perioperative outcome measurement.

The perioperative analgesic literature in breast reconstruction has largely addressed these areas in isolation. The author did not identify a prior review that mapped evidence across all five domains together, examined their interdependence, or described the evidence gap specific to breast implant revision surgery as a procedurally distinct subpopulation. The revision population is relevant because revision procedures are common after implant-based reconstruction, many patients have had prior chest wall radiation, and the psychosocial context of oncology-related revision surgery may create communication and measurement challenges that differ from primary reconstruction or non-oncologic breast surgery.

This review maps evidence across five perioperative analgesic decision domains, summarizes that evidence by domain using plain study-design descriptions, and describes evidence gaps relevant to implant revision surgery. Statements about implant revision are labeled throughout as evidence-gap analysis and inference.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Review Design

This is a narrative literature review with a documented search. It applies explicit search strings and eligibility criteria but does not include the methods of a systematic review: (1) a single author selected and summarized sources; (2) only one bibliographic database was searched, with supplementary targeted retrieval; (3) screening counts were not recorded; and (4) no formal risk-of-bias assessment, certainty rating, or quantitative pooling was performed. No reporting standard (such as PRISMA 2020) is claimed.

Section 8.5 presents an exploratory narrative observation about whether anesthetic background (propofol TIVA versus volatile anesthesia) might modify the measured opioid-sparing effect of PECS blocks. This is a hypothesis drawn from a reading of published trial abstracts and full texts where available. It is not a subgroup analysis or meta-analysis, and no data were pooled.

### 2.2 Review Questions

Primary: What is the scope and nature of published evidence for perioperative analgesic strategies across five clinical decision domains in adults undergoing breast reconstruction surgery?

Secondary: What are the evidence gaps and portability implications for breast implant revision surgery in each domain?

Exploratory (hypothesis-generating only): Is there a plausible reason to expect the opioid-sparing effect of PECS blocks to differ between propofol TIVA and volatile-anesthetic backgrounds, and can the published trials address this question?

### 2.3 Eligibility Criteria

Inclusion: English-language systematic reviews, meta-analyses, RCTs, prospective and cross-sectional studies, and society guidance documents; adults (≥18 years) undergoing breast reconstruction or breast surgery; perioperative analgesic strategy, patient communication, or postoperative recovery measurement in at least one of the five defined domains. Sources from adjacent populations (for example, thoracic surgery, arthroplasty, or general surgical and medical settings) were included where breast-specific evidence was lacking, and are labeled as indirect.

Exclusion: Case reports, case series (<20 patients), editorials, letters, and conference abstracts without full published data; pediatric populations; non-English publications.

### 2.4 Information Sources and Search Strategy

PubMed/MEDLINE was searched as the primary database, with no lower date limit. Targeted retrieval was conducted for society guidance (ERAS Society, ASRA Pain Medicine, ASA, PROSPECT, and the Society for Ambulatory Anesthesia [SAMBA]). Backward reference tracking from systematic reviews and meta-analyses was performed. Embase, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science were not searched as databases.

Sources were summarized narratively by decision domain, giving priority to systematic reviews and meta-analyses. No data were pooled. Where two meta-analyses addressed the same question (Section 4), their results are reported separately because they differ in included trials, comparators, and opioid units.

### 3.2 Principal Findings

The ERAS Society consensus review for breast reconstruction (Temple-Oberle et al., 2017) and the PROSPECT guideline for oncological breast surgery (Jacobs et al., 2020) both describe a multimodal, opioid-sparing framework that combines agents with different mechanisms to reduce reliance on any single drug class, including opioids. The ERAS review recommends anesthetic techniques that decrease PONV and pain; this review did not verify a specific recommendation for propofol TIVA over volatile anesthesia in that document. The evidence base for individual components varies substantially in breast-surgery specificity.

The Apfel simplified risk score uses four predictors: female sex, history of motion sickness or PONV, non-smoking, and postoperative opioid use (Apfel et al., 1999). In breast reconstruction, female sex is nearly universal; the other three factors vary by patient, and postoperative opioid use is directly influenced by the analgesic strategy chosen. The Apfel score was derived in patients receiving inhalational anesthesia for various surgery types and has not been specifically validated in breast reconstruction revision cohorts. The link between analgesic strategy and PONV risk is therefore plausible but has not been evaluated in a controlled trial specific to the revision population.

Consensus PONV guidelines address strategies to reduce baseline PONV risk, including the choice of anesthetic technique (Gan et al., 2020). In a mastectomy RCT in which both groups received a PECS block and propofol target-controlled infusion, opioid-free anesthesia with dexmedetomidine did not improve QoR-15 at 24 hours or postoperative analgesia compared with opioid-based anesthesia, but postoperative adverse events were significantly lower in the opioid-free group, and extubation was slower (Chen et al., 2025). This finding is consistent with the idea that a shared PECS block background limits the incremental analgesic benefit detectable between opioid-free and opioid-based strategies.

> **Summary — analgesic modality (narrative)**
> Society guidance for breast reconstruction and oncological breast surgery supports multimodal, opioid-sparing perioperative care that includes regional techniques and non-opioid adjuncts. Most supporting evidence comes from breast cancer surgery or mixed surgical populations rather than reconstruction revision, and effect sizes vary across studies and background regimens. The available evidence does not establish superiority over low-opioid conventional approaches for long-term functional outcomes.

### 3.3 Key Evidence Gaps

No RCT identified in this review evaluated analgesic modality as the primary comparison in elective breast reconstruction revision surgery. Most evidence derives from mastectomy or broader breast surgery populations. PONV estimates come from mixed populations that may not apply uniformly to revision contexts. Long-term functional outcomes are not characterized.

### 3.4 Evidence Gap — Implications for Breast Implant Revision Surgery

No primary evidence addresses analgesic modality in breast implant revision. The following implications are designated **EVIDENCE GAP — ANALYTICALLY INFERRED**. PONV reduction and opioid-sparing associated with propofol TIVA are mechanism-based and not procedure-specific, so they may carry over to revision surgery. However, revision surgery may involve a lower acute pain burden than primary reconstruction, which could reduce the absolute size of opioid-sparing differences compared with what has been observed in primary reconstruction or mastectomy trials. Prior chest wall radiation, common in post-mastectomy revision patients, may alter regional block efficacy and therefore the background against which TIVA-based opioid-sparing operates; this interaction has not been evaluated.

## 4. Results: Regional Analgesia — PECS Block, Serratus Anterior Plane Block, and Related Techniques

The PECS II ("modified Pecs") block, described in 2012, adds a deeper injection intended to block at least the pectoral nerves, the intercostobrachial nerve, intercostal nerves III–VI, and the long thoracic nerve (Blanco et al., 2012).

### 4.2 Principal Findings

PECS blocks reduce early postoperative opioid consumption and pain in breast cancer surgery compared with general anesthesia or systemic analgesia alone. Two meta-analyses address this question and are reported separately:

- Sun et al. (2020; 13 RCTs, 940 patients) reported a 24-hour weighted mean difference of −11.88 mg morphine equivalents (95% CI −15.50 to −8.26). Heterogeneity was very high (I² = 99.5%), and the Egger test suggested publication bias (P = .010), although the Begg test did not (P = .161).
- Hussain et al. (2019; 14 RCTs, 887 patients) reported a 24-hour weighted mean difference of −30.5 mg oral morphine (95% CI −42.2 to −18.8) for PECS II versus systemic analgesia, and found PECS II not statistically different from paravertebral block for 24-hour morphine consumption.

The two estimates differ in included trials, comparators, and opioid units (morphine equivalents versus oral morphine), so they should not be combined or treated as interchangeable. The direction of effect is consistent across both. The very high heterogeneity means that the effect in any individual patient or protocol cannot be predicted from either pooled estimate, and most included trials enrolled mastectomy rather than reconstruction or revision patients.

The network meta-analysis by An et al. (2023; 100 RCTs, 6,639 patients) found that several regional techniques, including PECS II, paravertebral, serratus anterior plane, erector spinae plane, and rhomboid intercostal blocks and local anesthetic infusion, were each associated with lower postoperative pain, 24-hour morphine consumption, and PONV compared with no block, but none reduced chronic pain. The authors judged the strength of evidence for their ranking to be very low. The SAMBA advisory (Ardon et al., 2022) recommends PECS blocks over systemic analgesia alone for breast-conserving surgery but could not recommend them over surgeon-performed local infiltration.

### 4.3 Key Evidence Gaps

Nearly all RCT evidence comes from breast cancer surgery or mixed breast surgery populations. The one RCT in implant surgery identified here studied primary aesthetic augmentation (Aarab et al., 2021). No adequately powered study was identified that evaluated PECS block efficacy specifically in breast implant revision, capsular contracture repair, or pocket revision. Optimal PECS technique parameters (volume, concentration, agent, adjuvant) are not standardized, which likely contributes to the heterogeneity in pooled analyses.

### 4.4 Evidence Gap — Implications for Breast Implant Revision Surgery

No direct evidence addresses PECS block efficacy in implant revision. The following are **EVIDENCE GAP — ANALYTICALLY INFERRED**. The PECS II block was originally described with tissue expanders and subpectoral prostheses among its main indications (Blanco et al., 2012), and an RCT in primary aesthetic augmentation found reduced pain and opioid use through day 5 (Aarab et al., 2021); this makes technique portability to implant revision plausible but unvalidated. Prior chest wall radiation, common in post-mastectomy revision patients, may alter fascial plane anatomy, local anesthetic spread, and therefore block coverage. A potentially lower acute pain burden in revision surgery relative to primary mastectomy could reduce the absolute analgesic demand against which block efficacy is measured.

### 5.2 Principal Findings

Meta-analyses in mixed surgical populations report opioid-sparing effects for dexamethasone, ketamine, alpha-2 agonists, magnesium, and IV lidocaine, and procedure-specific guidance for oncological breast surgery recommends paracetamol, NSAIDs, dexamethasone, and gabapentin (Jacobs et al., 2020). Effect sizes are generally small to moderate, and almost none of the underlying trials were conducted in breast reconstruction. For IV lidocaine, the benefit was concentrated in abdominal surgery and safety and dosing were flagged as unresolved (Vigneault et al., 2011). Because IV lidocaine is itself a local anesthetic, its use alongside large-volume fascial plane blocks raises the question of cumulative local anesthetic exposure, which is addressed in ASRA Pain Medicine LAST guidance (Neal et al., 2018).

A key interpretive issue across this domain is a possible ceiling effect: the incremental benefit of any adjuvant may be largest when background analgesic coverage is lowest, and smaller when regional block and TIVA already provide substantial coverage. Effect sizes from trials without regional block coverage may therefore not transfer directly to PECS-block-based pathways. In the Chen et al. (2025) RCT, which compared opioid-free with opioid-based anesthesia against a shared PECS block and propofol background, QoR-15 and postoperative analgesia did not differ at 24 hours. This is consistent with, but does not prove, a limited incremental contribution of opioid-free strategies above a PECS-inclusive background. This is an author inference.

### 5.3 Key Evidence Gaps

No adequately powered breast-reconstruction-specific RCT was identified that evaluated IV lidocaine against a contemporary PECS-block-plus-TIVA comparator. Magnesium lacks breast-surgery-specific controlled evidence in this context. Whether ketamine adds benefit for opioid-naive patients on PECS-plus-multimodal pathways has not been evaluated in a stratified breast surgery trial. Trials of multi-drug opioid-free "bundles" do not allow the effect to be attributed to individual agents.

### 6.2 Principal Findings: Health Literacy and Comprehension

The Institute of Medicine (2004) frames health literacy as the capacity to obtain, process, and understand basic health information needed to make appropriate health decisions. The AHRQ systematic review by Berkman et al. (2011) found low health literacy consistently associated with more hospitalizations, greater use of emergency care, lower receipt of some preventive services, and poorer ability to take medications appropriately and to interpret labels and health messages. These findings come from general medical settings; perioperative-specific outcomes were not the focus.

Consent documents frequently exceed recommended reading levels. Paasche-Orlow et al. (2003) found that consent-form template text provided by IRBs at US medical schools exceeded the schools' own readability standards, and Eltorai et al. (2015) found that invasive-procedure consent forms from Rhode Island hospitals were written at an average 15th-grade reading level.

The post-mastectomy revision population may face compounding barriers: prior oncologic encounters and treatments, the psychological burden of cancer history and body image concerns, and the cognitive-emotional load of a revision encounter that may carry uncertainty about reconstruction success. Teo et al. (2016) found that body image and quality of life vary with the timing and stage of reconstruction. Whether these factors affect comprehension during anesthetic counseling has not been studied directly (author inference).

### 6.3 Principal Findings: Shared Decision-Making and Preference Verification

The gap between expressed and informed preference is the central communication problem identified by the mapped evidence. Shared decision-making models depend on patients developing informed preferences (Elwyn et al., 2012), and clinicians may misjudge what patients prefer if preferences are not elicited directly (Mulley et al., 2012). Adding a repeat-back step to surgical consent produced a statistically significant but modest improvement in comprehension (Fink et al., 2010), and comprehension deficits persist in research consent despite a range of interventions (Cohn & Larson, 2007). These findings raise questions about whether adequate comprehension is consistently achieved within standard preoperative encounters, particularly for anesthetic option disclosure in oncology-related surgical settings.

> **Analytical inference (author)**
> A structural tension exists between the goal of shared decision-making, which requires meaningful preference elicitation, and the possibility that patient preferences about anesthetic options may be expressed without adequate understanding of the options. The perioperative-specific literature does not consistently address how to distinguish expressed preference from informed preference under the elevated cognitive and emotional load of oncology-related surgery. This conclusion is drawn by extension from shared decision-making and health literacy research, not from direct study of preference formation in breast reconstruction patients.

### 6.4 Evidence Gap — Implications for Breast Implant Revision Surgery

The communication and risk stratification evidence reviewed here comes largely from general medical settings, surgical and research consent, and, for pain catastrophizing, knee arthroplasty. The psychosocial complexity specific to post-mastectomy revision surgery has not been directly studied in a communication context and is inferred from adjacent research. No study of pain catastrophizing or preoperative opioid use as risk factors in breast reconstruction populations was identified, and the arthroplasty evidence may not be directly portable.

### 7.2 Principal Findings

Bowyer et al. (2014) document that postoperative quality of recovery lacks a universally accepted definition and that assessment tools vary substantially in scope. Standard perioperative metrics such as NRS pain scores, PONV incidence, and discharge criteria capture only part of recovery and do not directly measure cognitive clarity, fatigue, sleep quality, emotional equilibrium, or perceived progress.

Two PECS-block RCTs illustrate the problem. Kamiya et al. (2018) found that a PECS block improved early pain but not QoR-40 on day 1, and the authors suggested that factors such as rebound pain, not captured by early analgesia measures, may explain this. Chen et al. (2025) found no QoR-15 difference between opioid-free and opioid-based anesthesia against a shared PECS block background, despite a significant difference in adverse events. Together these findings suggest that standard recovery instruments may have limited sensitivity to analgesic-strategy differences in this population, although two trials are not proof of systematic instrument failure.

Breast-reconstruction-specific outcome research (Pusic et al., 2009; Teo et al., 2016) shows that satisfaction, body image, and psychosocial well-being are patient-relevant outcomes that vary with reconstruction timing and stage, dimensions not captured by standard perioperative instruments.

> **Analytical inference (author)**
> In the breast reconstruction revision population, where psychosocial complexity may be elevated and procedures vary widely, the gap between standard recovery metrics and patient-experienced recovery may be larger than in less complex surgical populations. This is inferred from documented measurement limitations in adjacent contexts; no controlled study has tested it in revision populations.

### 7.3 Evidence Gap — Implications for Breast Implant Revision Surgery

Patient-reported outcome data specific to the acute recovery phase of breast implant revision surgery were not identified. Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds for QoR-15 and BREAST-Q have not been established in high psychosocial complexity subgroups. A patient-anchored global measure such as the Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC) could help distinguish instrument failure from treatment failure in apparent non-responders; its use as a co-primary endpoint in breast reconstruction analgesic trials was not identified in this review.

## 8. Integrated Synthesis

### 8.1 The Layered Evidence Structure

The mapped evidence suggests that perioperative analgesic planning in breast reconstruction involves a layered decision structure in which each domain's evidence modifies the interpretation of the others. Analgesic modality sets the pharmacological background against which regional blocks and adjuvants operate. Regional analgesia determines the anatomical distribution of analgesia and may create a ceiling that limits the incremental benefit of adjuvants. Adjuvant pharmacology adds opioid-sparing within the space remaining above that ceiling. Risk stratification and communication quality determine whether patients can meaningfully participate in strategy selection. Recovery measurement determines whether any benefit is detectable with the instruments used. This framework is an author interpretation.

### 8.2 Distribution of Evidence Across Domains

The most robust evidence in this review is for regional analgesia: two meta-analyses of RCTs and a network meta-analysis in breast cancer surgery, with very high heterogeneity and mostly mastectomy populations. Society guidance (ERAS, PROSPECT, SAMBA) supports multimodal, opioid-sparing care. Adjuvant evidence comes mainly from meta-analyses in mixed or non-breast surgical populations. Communication evidence comes mainly from general medical, surgical-consent, and research-consent settings, with one multicenter RCT of comprehension verification. Outcome-measurement evidence consists of instrument development studies, reviews, and two breast surgery RCTs whose recovery scores did not change. Evidence for predicting risk (for example, the Apfel score or pain catastrophizing in arthroplasty) is generally stronger than evidence that acting on those predictions changes outcomes in this population.

### 8.3 Cross-Domain Integration

> **Cross-domain observation — analytically inferred**
> The perioperative literature tends to treat analgesic strategy, patient communication, and recovery measurement as separate research domains. This separation may be limiting in the breast reconstruction revision context. If the ceiling-effect hypothesis in Section 8.5 is correct, the incremental benefit of each additional analgesic component would be smaller when the preceding layers are more effective, a relationship not captured when agents are studied in isolation. A patient who cannot meaningfully participate in analgesic decision-making because of health literacy barriers cannot generate valid preference data for personalizing strategy, and a recovery measure that does not capture patient-relevant dimensions cannot show whether a communication-informed decision produced a valued outcome. Prospective studies that integrate these domains would address this gap.

### 8.4 Evidence Gaps and Research Priorities

No RCT or adequately designed observational study identified in this review evaluated any perioperative analgesic domain with breast implant revision as the primary population. Three research priorities follow from the mapped evidence: (1) a revision-specific RCT of PECS block against a contemporary propofol TIVA background, with patient-valued recovery outcomes such as QoR-15 and PGIC as co-primary endpoints; (2) controlled studies with pre-specified risk stratification (for example, opioid tolerance, pain catastrophizing, and psychosocial comorbidity) in breast reconstruction; and (3) prospective validation of MCID thresholds for QoR-15 and BREAST-Q in high psychosocial complexity subgroups.

### 8.5 Exploratory Observation: Anesthetic Background as a Possible Modifier of PECS Block Opioid-Sparing (Hypothesis Only)

This section is a narrative, hypothesis-generating observation. No subgroup analysis, meta-analysis, risk-of-bias assessment, or data extraction for pooling was performed, and none of the numbers below were calculated by the author.

The two meta-analyses in Section 4 report 24-hour opioid reductions of −11.88 mg morphine equivalents (Sun et al., 2020) and −30.5 mg oral morphine (Hussain et al., 2019), with very high heterogeneity in the first. Neither publication's abstract reports a subgroup analysis by anesthetic maintenance technique. One possible contributor to heterogeneity is the anesthetic background: under propofol TIVA, which is associated with less PONV, there may be less residual opioid demand for a PECS block to reduce than under volatile anesthesia. This is a mechanistic hypothesis only.

The individual trials reviewed here can only partly inform it:

| Trial | Anesthetic background (as reported) | Comparison | Relevant finding (as reported) | Informs the hypothesis? |

These trials do not allow the hypothesis to be tested: only one PECS-versus-no-block trial clearly used a TIVA background, and its opioid outcome was intraoperative. Testing the hypothesis would require either a pre-specified subgroup analysis by anesthetic maintenance technique within an updated meta-analysis, using full-text trial data, or a trial randomizing PECS block versus no block within a propofol TIVA background. If the hypothesis were correct, the observable benefit of PECS blocks in TIVA-based pathways might appear more in early pain intensity and rescue-analgesia use than in large differences in opioid quantity.

## 9. Limitations

### 9.1 Search Limitations

PubMed/MEDLINE was the only bibliographic database searched. An earlier draft described accessing EMBASE through the Cochrane Library; because the Cochrane Library does not provide a full Embase search, this is not counted as an Embase search here. Embase, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science were not searched. When re-run on 26 October 2026, several MeSH terms in the documented search strings were not recognized by PubMed as written, and the Domain 1 string returned no records, so the documented search did not function as intended. Sources were also identified through guideline retrieval and backward reference tracking. The review is therefore not exhaustive. Any future systematic review would need validated MeSH terms, multiple databases, and recorded screening counts.

### 9.2 Single-Author Selection

Sources were selected and summarized by one author without duplicate screening, and screening counts were not recorded. This increases the risk of selection bias.

### 9.3 No Formal Risk-of-Bias or Certainty Assessment

No formal risk-of-bias tool or certainty framework was applied. Strength of evidence is described only by study design and directness, which does not account for individual study quality.

### 9.4 Exploratory Hypothesis

The anesthetic-background hypothesis in Section 8.5 was not pre-specified and was not tested. It is offered as a question for future research only.

### 9.5 Ceiling Effects and Portability of Effect Sizes

Much of the adjuvant pharmacology literature evaluates interventions against comparators without the regional block and propofol TIVA coverage used in some contemporary pathways. Effect sizes from these trials may overestimate the incremental benefit of adjuvants in block-based pathways. This is an inference that has not been tested directly.

### 9.6 Implant Revision Indirectness

Nearly all primary evidence comes from breast cancer surgery, mixed breast surgery, or non-breast populations; the only implant-surgery RCT identified studied primary aesthetic augmentation. No primary evidence specific to breast implant revision was identified. All conclusions about revision surgery are inferred, and the size, and in some cases the direction, of effects in revision surgery cannot be established from the available evidence.

### 9.7 Author Qualification

The author is a premedical student without clinical licensure. Interpretations reflect educational engagement with published sources rather than professional clinical judgment. This manuscript is not clinical guidance and must not be used as such.

## 10. Conclusion

This narrative literature review mapped published evidence for perioperative analgesic strategies across five clinical decision domains in adults undergoing breast reconstruction surgery and described evidence gaps relevant to breast implant revision surgery.

The most robust evidence concerns regional analgesia: two meta-analyses of RCTs in breast cancer surgery report that PECS blocks reduce early postoperative opioid consumption and pain, with very high heterogeneity and mostly mastectomy populations. The author proposes, as an untested hypothesis, that this effect may be smaller under a propofol TIVA background. Meta-analyses in mixed surgical populations support small-to-moderate opioid-sparing effects for several adjuvants, with little breast-specific evidence. Communication research documents gaps in comprehension and consent-form readability in general and surgical settings, and recovery-measurement research shows that standard instruments may miss patient-relevant dimensions.

No direct evidence on breast implant revision was identified in any domain. Conclusions about revision surgery in this review are evidence-gap inferences, not established findings. Three research priorities follow: a revision-specific RCT of PECS block versus no block against a propofol TIVA background, with QoR-15 and PGIC as co-primary endpoints; controlled studies with pre-specified risk stratification in breast reconstruction; and prospective MCID validation for QoR-15 and BREAST-Q in high psychosocial complexity subgroups.
